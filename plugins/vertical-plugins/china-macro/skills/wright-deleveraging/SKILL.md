## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Risk relocation, not risk removal
Every Chinese de-risking push moves risk to the next available channel. The 2013 WMP squeeze led to a "flight to risk" and shadow-banking growth. The 2016–18 shadow crackdown led to developer presales, mortgages and a bigger property bubble. Liability-side (funding) risk became asset-side (credit) risk. **Always ask: who was borrowing through the squeezed channel, and what do they use instead?**

### 2. The financial system as political shock absorber
After 2008, banks rolled over loans to unproductive SOEs and localities to avoid defaults and unemployment: financial risk was traded for lower political risk. Deleveraging halved credit growth (bank assets about 17.5% to 6.8%), so the shock absorber is gone. Losses now land on firms, developers, home buyers and local budgets.

### 3. Moral hazard and implicit guarantees are the root cause
Investors assumed the state guaranteed **both borrowers** (SOEs, LGFVs, developers) **and lenders** (state banks, state-linked nonbanks). Monetary and regulatory tightening only suppresses leverage *temporarily*. While guarantees exist, leverage returns when policy eases. **Precipitating defaults is the most effective deleveraging tool and the most dangerous**, because each broken guarantee invites doubt about the next one.

### 4. Periphery-to-core contagion ladder
Defaults moved inward: P2P lenders (2018), small banks (Baoshang 2019, Jinzhou), local SOE bonds (Yongcheng Coal, November 2020), trusts (2020–21), private developers (Evergrande, 2021), home buyers' mortgage boycotts (July 2022). The next rungs are **LGFV public bonds** (about 40% of a RMB 33 trillion corporate bond market) and then banks.

### 5. Campaign-style governance and the coalition word
The campaign was launched through signals: a May 2016 *People's Daily* "authoritative personage" interview ("A tree cannot grow to the sky"), then Politburo readouts, PBOC report language, Xinhua series and the 2017 National Financial Work Conference. "Deleveraging" held a coalition together because technocrats (credit/GDP), SOE advocates (SOE debt) and planners (debt service) each read it their own way. Campaigns limit policy bandwidth.

### 6. Sequencing: monetary, then regulatory, then institutional
- **Monetary** (August 2016 to April 2018): quiet volatility first (14-day reverse repos, guidance to borrow longer, MLF), then 30 bp of hikes. It caused accidents (Sealand Securities, a policy-bank bond selloff, the NCD boom) and was insufficient on its own.
- **Regulatory** (2017–18) did the work: asset management rules (no guarantees, mark-to-market, one layer of intermediation, no restricted sectors), NCDs inside interbank caps, the LMR, the 334 checks, WMP subsidiaries.
- **Institutional**: the FSDC (2017) and CBIRC (2018) ended regulatory whack-a-mole.
- Once regulation held, monetary policy could **ease** from 2018.

### 7. Measure credit with bank assets, not just TSF
TSF misses relabeled shadow assets (claims on banks and NBFIs, investment receivables). The PBOC revised TSF **four times** in 2018–19, adding ABS, write-offs and central government bonds, which smoothed away the slowdown. Use bank-asset growth and the swing in **claims on banks + NBFIs** (+RMB 9 trillion a year in 2015–16, −RMB 3.1 trillion in 2018). Use **interest on credit vs nominal GDP** (interest larger every year since 2012) to judge credit traction.

### 8. The presale substitution (the campaign's biggest surprise)
Cut off from trusts, developers sold unbuilt homes and used buyers' mortgage-funded cash to repay shadow loans. In 2018, starts rose 20% while completed-home sales fell 26%. Presale revenue grew from RMB 5.8 trillion (2015) to RMB 16.3 trillion (June 2021). Property's share of bank loans rose from 24% to 33%. This is a **Ponzi-type, household-funded credit line**, and its reversal *was* the 2021–22 crash. Escrow existed but local governments did not enforce it.

### 9. Who gets squeezed: guarantees decide
SOEs are rate-insensitive and guaranteed, so tightening hits private firms first: negative net bond issuance after 2018, though private firms provide about 80% of urban jobs. Low-income households lose P2P credit, which reverses **financial deepening**. Moral hazard broke for peripheral assets but **survived for central SOEs and LGFVs**, so credit flowed to the least efficient borrowers. LGFV debt rose from RMB 24 trillion (2016) to 57 trillion (2021).

### 10. Beijing's scorecard
Credit growth and debt-to-GDP: **success** (credit/GDP 261% to 250%). Shadow and WMP risk: **success with a caveat** (credit risk and the property bubble grew). Local debt: **failure**. SOE leverage: **failure** (perpetual bonds). Overall: "highly necessary… but insufficient". The campaign marked **the peak of China's credit-driven growth model**.

### 11. Centralization changes the guarantee
Under collective leadership, many pro-stability voices made guarantees credible. Under Xi's centralization, guarantees depend on his preferences ("houses are for living, not for speculation", "common prosperity"). Signals from lower-level reformers lose clout, crackdowns get sharper, and bubbles unwind faster. **Read the leader to price the guarantee.**

### 12. Four options, a best path, six outcomes
- **Options**: (1) double down on deleveraging, (2) save local governments through fiscalization or monetization, (3) save property, (4) quietly abandon deleveraging, giving a short boom and stress within 1–2 years. **The tell is the GDP target.**
- **Best path**: credit growth only slightly above nominal GDP; fiscalize about RMB 54 trillion (45% of GDP) of local debt; fiscal support for household consumption; liberalize onshore markets to attract offsetting inflows.
- **Outcomes**: single-digit credit growth; weaker commodity imports (property at about 40–50% of the prior decade's level); falling rates, possibly with PBOC balance-sheet support; central–local fiscal restructuring; long-run CNY depreciation (98% of deposits in RMB, reserves about 8% of M2); persistent trade surpluses.

### 13. Crisis tools Beijing won't openly use
Liquidity facilities, forced restructurings and market interventions exist, but using them visibly would admit the growth model failed. **Expect quiet stress management.** "Push will never come to shove."
