# Chapter 2: A Tree Cannot Grow to the Sky

## Core Idea
After 2008, China's credit boom was the largest in at least a century, about 32% of global GDP through 2016. Shadow banks emerged to keep credit flowing to guaranteed but non-performing borrowers and to evade quotas. The result turned a stable, repressed bank system into a fragile, moral-hazard-driven one that Beijing could no longer see or steer.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Shadow banking as loan-preservation machinery**: shadow banks existed mainly so banks could keep loans to SOEs and LGFVs "performing" without marking them down.
  - How (the bridge-loan rollover): (1) a borrower's bank loan comes due; (2) an NBFI provides a short, high-rate bridge loan; (3) the bridge repays the old loan; (4) the bank grants a larger new loan on the basis of the "clean" repayment history; (5) the new loan repays the bridge plus interest. Loan officers sometimes ran the bridge vehicles themselves.
- **Channel business**: bank → WMP → trust, brokerage or asset-management plan → final borrower. The bank holds a claim on a nonbank, not a loan, so it carries lower risk weights and capital charges and can lend to restricted borrowers.
- **From financial repression to moral hazard**: before 2012 deposits were captive and stable, and the system was inefficient but crisis-resistant. By 2016 marginal funding came from WMPs and interbank borrowing, banks competed on rates, and *both sides* of bank balance sheets were riskier, on the assumption that the state would absorb losses.
- **Regulatory whack-a-mole**: the CBRC, CSRC and CIRC each saw only part of the system. When one channel was banned (for example bank–trust cooperation in 2010, or Circular 127 in April 2014), activity moved to another regulator's institutions or another asset label. Regulators also *wanted* their sectors to grow.
- **"The great ball of money"**: stable, low money-market rates, which the PBOC kept low to avoid a repeat of 2013, encouraged leveraged searches for yield. The money moved from property (2012–14) to equities (2014–15) to commodities and bonds (2016), along with P2P lending.

## Key Concepts
- **WMPs**: short-maturity (under a year), implicitly guaranteed pooled products. The balance was RMB 29.15 trillion in June 2022.
- **NCDs**: uncollateralized interbank certificates (from end-2013) that let even tiny banks borrow nationally. They were initially excluded from the one-third cap on interbank liabilities.
- **Trusts**: the only nonbanks with full lending licenses. 71 licenses were issued and 68 trusts remain, with about RMB 21 trillion AUM. They were the largest shadow channel.
- **Implicit guarantees**: the belief that SOE debt, LGFV debt, bank debt and WMPs cannot default.
- **Interest on credit vs nominal GDP**: estimated annual interest on outstanding credit has exceeded nominal GDP growth every year since 2012. IMF estimate: RMB 6.5 trillion of new credit added RMB 5 trillion to GDP in 2007–08, but RMB 20 trillion was needed for the same gain in 2015–16.
- **LGFV economics**: return on assets is under 2%. The bond swap cut LGFV borrowing costs from about 10% (shadow rates) to 3.5% (Lou Jiwei, December 2015). At 10%, new debt only services old debt.
- **Loan-to-deposit ratio**: capped at about 75%. Lending quotas, deposit-rate ceilings and lending-rate floors guaranteed banks a fixed margin.

## Mental Models
- Use **the interest-to-GDP test**: when interest on credit is larger than nominal GDP growth, new credit mostly services old debt and the credit impulse loses traction.
- Think of **high-rate, rate-insensitive borrowers** (developers with fat margins, LGFVs with no budget constraint) as the natural customers of high-yield WMP money. Their guarantees raised borrowing costs for the whole economy.
- Persistent **PPI deflation alongside positive CPI** (2012–16) is the signature of credit flowing to state producers who keep producing despite losses, to avoid layoffs.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading early shadow banking only as risk**: officials initially saw WMPs as interest-rate liberalization and P2P as financial inclusion. That ambiguity is why regulators tolerated them.
- **Regulating lenders instead of funding**: until 2018 controls hit the lending channels, not the WMP funding source, so activity simply relabeled itself.

## Worked Example: the 2015 equity bubble as a shadow-banking event
- Property weakened in 2014, so liquidity rotated into stocks. The Shanghai Composite surged from late 2014 without macro support.
- More than RMB 3 trillion of WMP money financed margin trading (Nie Qingping, ex-chairman of China Securities Finance Corp, CSFC). Investors pledged newly bought shares as collateral for more margin.
- On the way down, the same leverage amplified the selloff. The state bought about RMB 2 trillion of stocks via the CSFC, banned short selling and sent security forces to investigate.
- Index levels from the rescue were never regained. The rescue mainly let speculators exit, and money rotated back into housing and then, in April 2016, into commodity futures, whose volumes exceeded all Chinese equity exchanges.

## Key Takeaways
1. Between 2012 and 2016, informal lending supplied RMB 36.7 trillion, about 31% of new credit, much of it to developers, LGFVs and speculators.
2. Shadow banking's real functions were evading quotas, hiding NPLs, saving capital and lending to restricted sectors.
3. The PBOC's own rate-smoothing after 2013 subsidized leveraged speculation.
4. By 2016 Beijing was losing its main policy lever, the allocation of credit, and resembled the pre-2008 US, with SPV and SIV-like vehicles.
5. The financial system had stopped absorbing political risk and started *creating* it.
