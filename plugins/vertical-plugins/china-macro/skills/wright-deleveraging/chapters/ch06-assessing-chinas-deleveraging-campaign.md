# Chapter 6: Assessing China's Deleveraging Campaign

## Core Idea
Judged on Beijing's own objectives, the campaign succeeded at slowing credit and defusing shadow-banking funding risk. It failed on local-government and SOE debt, and it amplified property risk. It ended the credit-driven growth model without building a replacement. Centralization under Xi made the guarantees that underpinned stability contingent on one person's preferences.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two interpretations of China's growth** (decide which one the evidence supports):
  - **"Froth" view**: shadow-financed speculation was a mistake. Remove it and growth returns to a sustainable pace with lower borrowing costs.
  - **"Intrinsic" view**: credit growth under moral hazard *was* the growth model. Remove it and growth structurally slows.
  - Wright's evidence favors the intrinsic view: the contraction "limited credit to real borrowers, not just speculative froth."
- **Four camps of published assessments**:
  1. **Success**: Orlik, *The Bubble That Never Pops* ("Deleveraging without Self-Detonating"), and the CBIRC (December 2020).
  2. **Overdone**: Xu Zhong (PBOC) said local-debt cleanup was "too hasty"; Xu Gao (Everbright); NIFD/CASS warned of a balance-sheet recession.
  3. **Didn't happen**: Leland Miller (China Beige Book) and Christopher Balding argued credit just moved into other instruments.
  4. **Balanced or retrospective**: Shuli Ren (Bloomberg) said China became "a much safer place to put your money"; Hung Tran (ex-IMF).
- **Beijing's-terms scorecard**: see the Worked Example.
- **Liability-side safety vs asset-side risk**: the campaign removed run risk (WMP rollover) and created credit risk (borrowers who could not refinance). Wright's *Credit and Credibility* thesis is that **paring back guarantees is the most probable path to a systemic crisis**, because investors cannot tell where Beijing's tolerance for defaults ends.
- **Partial moral-hazard break**: guarantees broke for P2P lenders, trusts, small banks, private developers and some local SOEs, but *held* for central SOEs and LGFVs. Credit therefore concentrated in the least efficient "safe" borrowers, which strengthened the SOE role.
- **Collective vs centralized guarantees**: under collective leadership, many voices for stability made guarantees credible. Under centralization, guarantees depend on perceptions of Xi's preferences ("houses are for living, not for speculation"; "common prosperity"), which accelerated the unwinding.

## Key Concepts
- **Financial system size**: RMB 377 trillion in bank assets, more than half of world GDP. Growth above 15% would mean new credit of about 50% of GDP every year, so it is impossible.
- **LGFV bond market exposure**: LGFV bonds are about 40% of the RMB 33 trillion corporate bond market. LGFV public-bond defaults are the key unfired risk.
- **Credit/GDP** (weighted adjusted TSF and bank assets): 172% (2008), 261% (end-2016), 250% (2018), then up again with Covid in 2020.
- **TSF revisions**: the PBOC changed the TSF methodology four times in 2018–19, each time boosting the headline. It added ABS and loan write-offs (July 2018) and central government bonds (December 2019).
- **Shadow assets (CBIRC)**: down RMB 16 trillion on the broad measure from end-2017 to end-2019, and down about RMB 12 trillion from peak on the narrow measure.
- **LGFV interest-bearing debt plus payables**: RMB 24.1 trillion (2016) to 56.7 trillion (2021).
- **Infrastructure FAI**: 19% (2017) to 3.8% (2018), which prompted Beijing to relax implicit-debt pressure by end-2018.
- **Perpetual bonds**: SOE issuance surged in late 2018 and early 2019. They count as equity but carry higher interest, so they are not real deleveraging.
- **Financial deepening reversal**: shadow banks had been the main channel to small firms and low-income households. The crackdown cut them off.

## Mental Models
- **Necessary but insufficient**: the campaign removed near-term risk but laid no foundation for sustainable growth.
- **Risk is conserved; it changes form**. Always ask what new risk a de-risking step creates.
- **Under centralization, read the leader, not the bureaucracy**, to price guarantees.
- **There is no magic solution to deflate an unprecedented credit bubble.** Policymakers cannot control market outcomes, even in a state-controlled system.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging deleveraging by headline TSF**: revisions smoothed away the slowdown.
- **Calling SOE leverage targets "met"** when they were met through perpetual bonds.
- **Assuming Beijing could have acted in 2013**: there was no political constituency for it under consensus decision-making.

## Worked Example: the scorecard on Beijing's own objectives
| Objective | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Slow credit; cap debt-to-GDP | Bank asset growth roughly halved; credit/GDP 261% → 250% | ✅ Success, though politically hard to sustain |
| Remove shadow-banking and WMP systemic risk | Mark-to-market; WMP reliance down; shadow assets −RMB 12–16 trillion | ✅ Success, with a caveat: credit risk shifted to assets and the property bubble re-inflated |
| Reduce local-government debt pressure | LGFV debt RMB 24.1 trillion → 56.7 trillion; infrastructure growth collapsed | ❌ Failure |
| Cut SOE debt and improve efficiency | Debt-for-equity swaps stalled; perpetual bonds; SOEs crowded out private firms | ❌ Failure |

## Key Takeaways
1. The campaign marked the **peak of China's credit-driven growth model**. A return to about 15% credit growth is unrealistic.
2. It made a June-2013-style funding crisis much less likely, and P2P collapses never reached core banks.
3. It created asset-side credit risk and an unexpectedly larger property bubble.
4. Surviving guarantees (central SOEs, LGFVs) misallocate credit to the least efficient borrowers.
5. Consumption-led growth will be slower than investment-led growth because household income is only about 60% of GDP.
6. Political centralization amplified the transmission of both tightening and crackdowns.
