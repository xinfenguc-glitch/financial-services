# Chapter 7: China's Upcoming Policy Choices and Implications for the United States

## Core Idea
After deleveraging, neither property nor local-government infrastructure can drive growth. Credit will grow more slowly than the economy, demographics have turned, and Beijing is losing fiscal control as LGFVs strain. Wright lays out **four strategic options**, a **recommended four-part path**, **six probable long-run outcomes**, and a US policy stance: regulate US markets transparently and let Beijing win or lose foreign capital on its own.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four strategic options** (none assumes deep structural reform; reforms since 2013 were repeatedly launched and then reversed):
  1. **Doubling down on deleveraging**: new limits on local governments, SOEs and shadow banks; continued property curbs. *Tell*: lower GDP targets or dropping them. *Outcome*: sharply slower growth and lost international influence, but credibility with markets.
  2. **Saving local governments**: explicit recognition and **fiscalization** of local debt, possibly monetized via the PBOC balance sheet, while property and shadow-bank pressure is maintained. *Outcome*: infrastructure stabilizes, policymakers look reactive, and fiscal space shrinks.
  3. **Saving the property sector**: relax developer borrowing limits, provide liquidity, cut down payments and mortgage rates, cut resale taxes, subsidize first homes, keep growth targets near 5%. *Outcome*: likely ineffective structurally, because of high ownership, declining entrants and developers repairing balance sheets. Credibility is lost.
  4. **Abandoning deleveraging (unannounced)**: drop credit limits and quotas, aid local governments, developers and home buyers, raise growth targets to 6–7%, maybe relax capital ratios. *Outcome*: a short boom like March 2020, then **severe financial stress within 1–2 years**.
- **Beijing's best options** (Wright's recommendation):
  1. Keep credit growth **only marginally above nominal GDP growth**.
  2. **Restructure and fiscalize local-government debt**, about RMB 54 trillion (45% of GDP): guarantee LGFV debt, swap it into central bonds, then phase out or centralize LGFVs.
  3. Use **fiscal resources to raise household consumption**: transfers, a stronger safety net and health care, lower contributions, and a higher household income share.
  4. **Liberalize onshore markets** to attract inflows that offset inevitable household and corporate diversification outflows, and keep the FX rate stable.
- **Six probable long-run outcomes**:
  1. The slowdown in credit and investment continues, with single-digit credit growth.
  2. Imports of iron ore, copper, aluminum and refined products weaken. Property settles at about 40–50% of the previous decade's level.
  3. Interest rates on government and quasi-government debt fall, with central-bank funding involved.
  4. Central–local fiscal roles are restructured, including new local revenues and possibly property taxes.
  5. The CNY depreciates over the long term.
  6. Chinese trade surpluses and developed-market deficits persist.
- **Half-reformed interest rates**: lending rates were liberalized in 2015, but deposit rates are still effectively set through informal bank coordination and the benchmark. Banks cannot cut lending rates enough to revive demand, and rate *hikes* fall on private borrowers because SOEs are rate-insensitive.
- **Credibility under centralization**: reform signals from below no longer carry weight. Only Xi can credibly change course, and one speech can reprice a sector. The 2021 campaign (Didi's IPO reversed, Alibaba, Meituan, Tencent, the July 2021 education ban) and the end of zero-Covid (late November 2022) illustrate this.
- **Crisis tools you can't use**: emergency liquidity, forced restructurings and market intervention exist, but open use admits the model failed. Expect stress to be managed quietly. "Push will never come to shove."

## Key Concepts
- **Demographics**: 9.6 million births in 2022, against 17.9 million in 2016 and 10.6 million in 2021. The population declined in 2022, and the working-age population peaked in 2013.
- **PBOC 2019 stress test**: the "severely adverse" GDP scenario was 4.15%, and official growth fell below it in 2020 and 2022. The pandemic-era average was 4.5%.
- **Goal**: double per-capita GDP between 2020 and 2035.
- **2023 leadership**: Premier Li Qiang. He Lifeng replaced Liu He as economics vice-premier. The PBOC and MoF heads were kept for continuity.
- **Land sales**: −24% (about −RMB 1.6 trillion) in 2022. LGFVs made 40–45% of 2022 land purchases, propping up local revenue.
- **FX math**: about 98% of deposits are in RMB. Reserves of $3.13 trillion (December 2022) equal about 8% of money supply, the lowest in at least three decades and below 1997 levels. The PBOC will defend the currency selectively.
- **US exposure**: the Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act (HFCAA, 2020) and PCAOB inspections; 262 Chinese firms listed in the US (September 2022); US holdings of Chinese securities about $1.2 trillion (end-2020).
- **1998 precedent**: the Central Financial Work Commission under Zhu Rongji drove bank restructuring. Reports in 2023 said it could be revived.

## Mental Models
- **Map every policy headline to one of the four options**, then read the GDP target as the tell.
- **Low rates lead to a weaker CNY**. Debt management requires falling rates, which pushes capital outward and weakens the currency.
- **Slower investment brings bigger trade surpluses**. Every meaningful investment slowdown has widened China's surplus.
- **Portfolio flows are not leverage for Beijing**. Unlike FDI and supply chains, they give China little political leverage.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming structural reform is the base case**: it has been promised since 2013 and repeatedly reversed.
- **US policy that steers investors away from Chinese assets**: it hands Beijing a scapegoat, can backfire (late-Trump military-linked investment bans drew in third-party buyers) and costs US investors diversification.
- **Expecting property to drive growth again**: at most it contributes cyclically.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fiscalizing local debt and lowering rates are near-term imperatives, and they imply a weaker yuan.
2. Commodity exporters should plan for structurally lower Chinese construction demand.
3. Watch GDP targets and leadership rhetoric, not technocrats, for direction.
4. The US interest is transparent regulation of its own markets, not steering flows.
5. China's financial system can no longer serve as the political system's shock absorber.
