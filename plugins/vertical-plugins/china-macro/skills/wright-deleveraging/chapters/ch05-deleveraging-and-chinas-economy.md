# Chapter 5: Deleveraging and China's Economy

## Core Idea
Beijing hoped to "skim the froth" of speculation without hurting real borrowers. Instead, halving credit growth hit output, private firms, consumers and local finances. Most surprisingly, it **re-inflated the property bubble**, because developers replaced shadow loans with presale cash from mortgaged home buyers.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The five real-economy effects** (use as a checklist):
  1. Growth slowed more than intended. Official GDP hid it, but output contracted in 2H 2018.
  2. The property bubble re-inflated through household mortgages and presales.
  3. The private sector was squeezed most, while SOEs kept credit.
  4. Inflation fell. Core CPI has stayed around 1% since 2018 as low- and middle-income households lost shadow credit.
  5. Local-government fiscal strain rose, and special revenue bonds (SRBs) became the stopgap.
- **Developer survival model (2017–18)**: (a) slow construction of already-sold units to save cash; (b) push presales of not-yet-built units to raise cash. Starts surge while completions fall. This introduced **Ponzi-type financing**: presale cash went to repay legacy shadow loans that were being called in.
- **Credit reallocation to households**: denied high-yield shadow assets, banks sought the next best yield, which was mortgages and consumer credit, amplified by shantytown redevelopment. Household debt/GDP rose while corporate debt/GDP fell.
- **Credit asymmetry for private firms**: credit expansions help private firms slowly (through spillovers), but credit crunches hurt them immediately. *Guo jin min tui*: "the state advances, the private sector retreats."
- **Smoothed GDP vs alternative activity data**: verify official growth with output series, the Rhodium China Activity Tracker (R-CAT, 103 industrial series), and Fed-style credit-impulse indicators.

## Key Concepts
- **Credit growth (bank assets)**: 15.7% (2015–16), 8.4% (2017), 6.8% (2018).
- **GDP smoothing**: 6.9% (Q1 2018) to 6.5% (Q4 2018). Growth stayed within 6.5–7.1% throughout 2015–18, the smoothest of any major economy in 30–40 years.
- **2018 slump evidence**: industrial value-added slowed from 7.0% (April) to 5.4% (November). Auto sales fell 16% (November–December), white goods contracted, completions fell 8%, and R-CAT showed weighted output down 6–7% y/y in Q3 2018.
- **Presales divergence (2018)**: new starts +20%, completed-unit sales −26% (floor space), presales +10% by floor space and +22% by revenue.
- **Presale revenue**: RMB 5.8 trillion (end-2015) to a peak of RMB 16.3 trillion (June 2021).
- **Property share of bank loans**: 24% (2014) to 33% (2018), counting developer loans plus mortgages.
- **New household loans**: RMB 7.3 trillion in 2018 against RMB 8.3 trillion of corporate loans, then RMB 6–8 trillion a year in 2019–21.
- **Vanke vs Evergrande (2018)**: Vanke's Yu Liang said his goal was to "survive". Evergrande kept borrowing and buying land, and the most aggressive 2017–18 leveragers were the first to default in 2021–22.
- **Private-firm bonds**: net issuance negative most months after 2018, while SOE net issuance stayed high. Private firms provide about 80% of urban employment.
- **Household income**: about 55–60% of GDP. Disposable income after taxes and contributions is under 50%.
- **Infrastructure FAI growth**: 3.8% (2018, 2019), 0.9% (2020), 0.4% (2021), then a rebound in 2022.
- **Fund budget**: land-sale revenues and related spending. It went into deficit for the first time in 2015. SRBs fund the gap, and about 20–30% of 2018–22 SRB issuance went to refinancing.
- **Yongcheng Coal (November 2020)**: Henan redirected funds away from bondholders, and no Henan issuer could sell bonds for over a month. Local governments became a *source* of risk.

## Mental Models
- Think of **presales as a hidden, household-funded credit line to developers**. Its reversal *is* the 2021–22 credit crunch.
- Use **starts minus completions** as a stress gauge. A widening gap means developers are prefunding with buyers' money.
- Use **core CPI, not headline CPI**, to read household credit and income health. Headline spikes have come from pork supply shocks.
- **Land sales lag developer distress by about a year**, because developers pay for land parcels over about 12 months.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming shadow credit only funded speculation**: it also funded real borrowers (localities, private SMEs, households), so cutting it cut activity.
- **Treating mortgage growth as safer than developer lending**: here it was the *channel* for developer leverage.
- **Lax escrow enforcement**: local governments wanted developers to expand, so presale protections went unenforced.

## Worked Example: tracing one yuan through the presale substitution
1. 2017: a trust calls in a 10% loan to Developer D as shadow channels close.
2. D launches presales on a newly started project. A buyer pays the full price, with a down payment plus a bank mortgage, and starts paying the mortgage immediately.
3. D uses the presale cash to repay the trust instead of keeping it in escrow for construction, and slows building on earlier projects.
4. On the bank's books, a "safe" mortgage replaces the trust exposure. On D's books, a shadow loan becomes an unfunded construction obligation to the household.
5. 2021–22: sales collapse after the three red lines and Covid. D cannot finish homes. Buyers threaten mortgage boycotts, which turns developer risk into household and bank risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. The real-economy hit (2018) was larger than official GDP showed. Use alternative indicators.
2. Deleveraging hurt the efficient private sector and protected inefficient SOEs.
3. The property bubble grew *because of* the campaign, through presales and mortgages.
4. Persistently low core inflation reflects weak household income and lost consumer credit.
5. Local governments lost land revenue and shadow credit, leaving SRBs, a narrow and partly refinancing-driven channel.
