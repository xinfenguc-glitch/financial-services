# Chapter 1: The Fear (plus Executive Summary)

## Core Idea
China's current distress (property collapse, local-government fiscal strain, rising defaults) traces back to one policy choice: the 2016 deleveraging campaign against shadow banks. That campaign was itself a delayed response to the fear created by the **June 2013 interbank crisis**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Reform-induced crisis ("ironically caused by a genuine effort at reform")**: both the 2013 liquidity squeeze and the 2021–22 property meltdown were triggered by attempts to *reduce* risk.
  - When to use: whenever Beijing tightens on a risky channel, look for where the displaced financing goes next.
  - How: (1) identify the channel being squeezed; (2) ask who was borrowing through it; (3) ask what they will use instead (2016–18 answer: home buyers' presale cash).
- **The financial system as political shock absorber**: China used banks to roll over loans to unproductive firms, avoiding bankruptcies and unemployment. Financial risk rose so political risk could fall. After deleveraging halved credit growth, the system can no longer play this role, so credit risk and political consequences surface in the real economy.
- **Periphery-to-core contagion**: credit risk started in the riskiest corner and moved inward. The sequence was P2P networks (2018), then Baoshang Bank (2019, the first bank failure in over two decades), Yongcheng Coal, a local SOE (Nov 2020), trusts (2020–21), developers (2021) and finally home buyers threatening mortgage boycotts (2022).
- **Two competing narratives ("competition of systems")**:
  - **Technocratic competence**: Beijing pre-empted a crisis, which vindicates the one-party model.
  - **Self-made trap**: China reverted to the emerging-market mean after a credit boom, which discredits the model.
  - The deleveraging campaign is the best test case for deciding between them.

## Key Concepts
- **Presale (preconstruction) housing**: apartments sold and mortgaged before they are built. China had escrow rules, but local governments enforced them only selectively.
- **June 20, 2013**: interbank rates hit 20–30% after the PBOC told banks on June 17 to manage liquidity themselves. Stocks fell more than 10% in a day and a half. The PBOC injected liquidity late on June 20 and on June 25 promised "sufficient" liquidity.
- **"Mother bank"**: the market's view of the PBOC as the guaranteed lender of last resort.
- **Too big to fail (shadow products)**: after the PBOC blinked in 2013, WMPs and shadow products were treated as implicitly guaranteed.
- **Property-related lending**: about RMB 73 trillion ($10.7 trillion), covering mortgages, developer loans and informal credit.
- **Potential growth arithmetic**: the labor force has shrunk since 2013 and returns to property and infrastructure capital are falling. Without financial reform, growth heads toward 2% or below within a decade.

## Mental Models
- Think of every credit squeeze in China as **risk relocation, not risk removal**.
- Use **"who blinked last"** to price guarantees. Each time Beijing rescues a market, risk-taking in that market goes up afterward.
- Think of reform as **"slower growth now for different drivers later"**. Borrowers who rely on rollovers must be cut off, and their sectors must shrink.
- In a semi-reformed system, **state firms are rate-insensitive**. Tightening therefore squeezes private borrowers first, and the efficiency of investment *falls*.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the 2016–19 campaign as old news**: it is "the only natural starting point" for explaining the slowdown that followed.
- **Reading official 2022 GDP (3%) at face value**: actual growth was probably negative.
- **Placing Chinese reforms on a simple market-vs-state axis**: the consequences of reform are hard to predict in a system where state firms have soft budget constraints.

## Worked Example: anatomy of the June 2013 squeeze
1. **Setup.** Deposit-rate ceilings meant banks used WMPs (1–3 month products) to compete for funds and rolled them over continuously.
2. **Trigger.** On June 17 the PBOC told banks to manage their own maturity mismatches and withheld liquidity.
3. **Cascade.** Banks hoarded cash and interbank rates spiked above WMP yields. Investors (including other banks) stopped rolling over WMPs, so issuers had to borrow in a frozen market.
4. **Fire sale.** Everyone sold whatever was liquid, and equities fell more than 10% despite daily price limits.
5. **Capitulation.** The PBOC injected liquidity (June 20) and issued reassurance (June 25). Rates normalized, but the PBOC lost credibility.
6. **Lesson the market learned.** Shadow products are effectively guaranteed. WMP issuance accelerated through 2016.

## Key Takeaways
1. The 2016 campaign is the root of China's post-2017 slowdown and of its property crisis.
2. Each Chinese de-risking effort displaced risk somewhere else, and the next problem showed up where the financing migrated.
3. Default risk has travelled from the periphery to the core. Track the next ring (LGFV public bonds, then banks).
4. Long-run growth now depends on whether the financial system can reallocate credit from property and LGFVs to productive private firms.
5. Wright frames the campaign as a test of the Chinese state's adaptability.
