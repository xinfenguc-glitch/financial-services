# Summary — *Grasping Shadows: The Politics of China's Deleveraging Campaign*

**Logan Wright** · CSIS Trustee Chair in Chinese Business and Economics · April 2023 · 101 pp.

## The argument

China's structural slowdown began in 2016, not with Covid or Evergrande. That year Beijing launched a "deleveraging campaign" to shrink the shadow banking system. On its own narrow terms the campaign worked: shadow credit contracted, bank funding stabilized, and the risk of a 2013-style liquidity crisis fell. But it also cut credit growth roughly in half, and in doing so it ended the credit-and-investment growth model in place since 2008.

Risk did not disappear; it moved. Funding risk on banks' liabilities became credit risk on their assets. Property developers replaced shadow loans with presale cash from home buyers, which inflated the housing bubble that burst in 2021–22. The financial system can no longer act as the Party's political "shock absorber".

## How the shadow system grew (2009–2016)

The 2008 stimulus was nominally RMB 4 trillion but much larger in practice because banks funded it. It produced the largest single-country credit boom in a century: credit grew 32% in 2009, and bank assets doubled in four years. When Beijing tightened, local governments, state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and developers kept borrowing through shadow channels. Banks sold short-term wealth management products (WMPs), which worked like higher-rate deposits, and routed the money through trusts, brokerages and asset managers to restricted borrowers. These structures preserved capital, hid bad loans and rolled over credit that could not be repaid. From 2012 to 2016, informal lending supplied about 31% of new credit (RMB 36.7 trillion).

The engine was **moral hazard**: investors assumed the state guaranteed both borrowers and lenders. The June 2013 interbank crunch confirmed it. The People's Bank of China (PBOC) withheld liquidity, overnight rates hit 20–30%, and the central bank backed down within days. Markets learned the PBOC would blink. A "great ball of money" then rolled from property to equities (the 2015 crash) to commodities and bonds (2016). Meanwhile, each regulator's rule simply pushed activity into another regulator's jurisdiction.

## The campaign

A May 2016 *People's Daily* interview with an "authoritative personage", widely read as Liu He, set the tone: "A tree cannot grow to the sky." Politburo statements and the 2017 Financial Work Conference built consensus, which held because "deleveraging" meant different things to different camps: lower credit-to-GDP for technocrats, lighter SOE balance sheets, and less debt-service drag for planners. The tools escalated in four stages:

1. **Monetary tightening (from August 2016).** The PBOC injected rate volatility and raised policy rates by 30 bp. This caused side effects: the Sealand Securities scare, banks funding themselves with negotiable certificates of deposit (NCDs), and a policy-bank bond selloff. Tightening alone proved insufficient and dangerous.
2. **Regulatory tightening (2017–18).** The asset management rules ended WMP guarantees, required mark-to-market, limited intermediation to one layer and barred restricted sectors. NCDs became subject to interbank caps, a liquidity matching ratio penalized short-term funding, P2P lending was wound down, and SOEs got leverage targets.
3. **Reorganization.** A new Financial Stability and Development Committee and a merged bank-and-insurance regulator, the CBIRC, closed gaps between regulators.
4. **Local-government discipline.** Localities were told to disclose and repay "implicit debt".

Later steps included the "three red lines" for developers (2020) and trust-loan cuts (2021).

## What happened

Bank asset growth fell from about 17.5% to 6.8% (2018), and loans and deposits returned to bank balance sheets. But industrial output dropped 6–7% while official GDP barely moved. Private firms were squeezed while SOEs kept their credit, core inflation settled near 1%, and infrastructure investment stalled. Credit concentrated in the coastal southeast. Defaults climbed from the periphery inward: P2P (2018), small banks (2019), a local SOE (2020), trusts and developers (2021), then home buyers' mortgage boycotts (2022).

## Verdict on Beijing's own goals

| Goal | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Slow credit growth and the debt-to-GDP ratio | **Success.** Credit-to-GDP fell from 261% to 250%. |
| Contain shadow banking and WMP funding risk | **Success, with a caveat.** The property bubble re-inflated along the way. |
| Reduce local-government debt | **Failure.** Local financing vehicle (LGFV) debt rose from RMB 24 trillion to RMB 57 trillion. |
| Reduce SOE leverage | **Failure.** SOEs used perpetual bonds, which add debt service rather than cut it. |

Deleveraging also reversed **financial deepening** for small firms and poorer households. Moral hazard broke for most assets but survived for central SOEs and LGFVs, so credit flowed to the least efficient borrowers. Wright calls the campaign necessary but insufficient.

**Politics** amplified everything. The campaign ran under consensus-style technocratic governance. As power centralized under Xi Jinping, guarantees became contingent on his preferences, as in "houses are for living, not for speculation". Later campaigns therefore became sharper, and the unwinding of the bubbles accelerated.

## What comes next

Wright frames four options for Beijing: double down on deleveraging, bail out local governments, rescue property, or quietly abandon deleveraging (a short boom, then stress). His recommended path:

- keep credit growth only slightly above nominal GDP;
- restructure and fiscalize roughly RMB 54 trillion of local debt;
- use fiscal transfers to lift household consumption;
- open onshore markets to attract inflows that offset outflows.

He expects single-digit credit growth, weaker commodity imports, falling interest rates, central–local fiscal restructuring, a gradually weaker yuan and persistent trade surpluses.

For Washington, he advises transparent regulation of US markets and disclosure rules for Chinese issuers, not steering investors away from Chinese assets. Beijing has crisis tools, he concludes, but using them openly would admit the model failed, so stress will be managed quietly.
