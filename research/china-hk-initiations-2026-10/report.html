Cross-cutting themes

1. Strong H1 numbers, de-rated shares

Most of the 17 reported strong H1'26 results, yet the source pack notes every stock trades 28–81% below its 52-week high. China's CSI 300 fell 5.8% in September on worries about tighter policy in major economies and weak domestic demand. Several names (CATL, GigaDevice, Han's Laser, Sungrow) now trade at or near multi-year-low multiples on forward earnings.

2. The AI capex chain has moved down the stack

AI servers are pulling through demand for niche memory (GigaDevice), electronic glass-fibre cloth (China Jushi), PCB drilling equipment (Han's Laser), grid equipment (Sieyuan) and storage (CATL, Sungrow). The question for each is how long pricing power lasts once capacity responds. We are Buy where order books give visibility (Han's) and Hold where earnings already look near peak (GigaDevice, Jushi).

3. Lithium has turned again

China lithium carbonate fell 24% in September to ~RMB122k/t; Wood Mackenzie expects 2026 supply to grow ~28%, and a revised SMM inventory methodology added ~175kt to stock estimates. We model lower H2'26 and FY27 prices for Tianqi and Ganfeng, below broker forecasts in the source pack.

4. Strong CNY is a quiet earnings headwind

USD/CNH is ~6.69. Exporters with dollar receivables (Sany, Sungrow, Sieyuan) booked FX finance losses in H1'26, and dollar-priced commodity producers (Zijin) translate fewer renminbi per unit.

5. A/H spreads matter

Most H-shares trade at large discounts to their A-shares (JL Mag −46%, Ganfeng −45%, Tianqi −44%, Sany −18%), but CATL's H-share trades at a ~41% premium. Where both lines exist we name our preferred line in each report.

6. Robotics and AI models: real growth, stretched prices

Horizon Robotics and Edge Medical combine fast growth with valuations the market has already cut sharply, so we rate them Buy. Leaderdrive (Sell), Z.AI and MiniMax (Hold) price in outcomes several years away.

Company background

Zijin Mining grew out of the Zijinshan gold-copper deposit in Shanghang county, western Fujian, a low-grade orebody that state geologists had explored in the 1980s and that larger operators considered uneconomic. The company was formally established in 1993. Under geologist Chen Jinghe it pioneered large-scale heap leaching of low-grade ore, and Zijinshan became one of China's largest gold mines. That early experience shaped a culture that still defines the group: buy or develop deposits others overlook, then lower their cost through in-house geology, mining and metallurgical engineering. The company became a joint-stock company in 2000, listed H-shares in Hong Kong in 2003 and A-shares in Shanghai in 2008. Its largest shareholder is Minxi Xinghang, the investment vehicle of the Shanghang county government.

Overseas expansion began in the mid-2000s and accelerated after 2015 through counter-cyclical acquisitions bought when sellers were under pressure. These included a 49.5% stake in Ivanhoe's Kamoa-Kakula copper project in the DRC, Nevsun (owner of the Timok project) and the RTB Bor complex in Serbia, Continental Gold's Buriticá mine in Colombia, Guyana Goldfields, Neo Lithium's 3Q brine in Argentina, and Rosebel in Suriname. At home, the Julong copper project in Tibet became a growth engine. In 2025 the group spun its overseas gold assets into Zijin Gold International, listed in Hong Kong, which it still controls. Zijin now operates on five continents and targets a global top-three position in both gold and copper by 2028.

The business

Zijin is an integrated miner. It owns and operates mines, processes ore into concentrate, doré or cathode, and runs smelting and refining plants that also treat third-party material. Revenue therefore mixes two very different businesses. Self-mined gold, copper, zinc, silver and lithium carry high margins and generate the large majority of gross profit. Refining and trading of purchased material (about 19% of H1'26 revenue) adds volume but earns thin margins. The jump in group gross margin from 15.8% in FY23 to 34.6% on a TTM basis reflects both higher metal prices and a rising share of self-mined output.

Its competitive edge is cost and capital efficiency. Zijin's in-house exploration teams, mine designers and metallurgists let it value deposits independently, build mines with its own engineering arm, and run difficult or low-grade orebodies profitably. Management describes this as an integrated 'ore-flow' model linking geology, mining, processing and refining. The company is willing to operate in frontier jurisdictions such as the DRC, Serbia, Colombia, Suriname and Tibet, where Western majors often hesitate, and Chinese policy-bank and bond-market funding lowers its cost of capital.

Copper is the strategic priority. Mined copper was 1.09Mt in 2025 and is targeted at 1.2Mt in 2026 and 1.5–1.6Mt by 2028, led by Julong (Tibet), the Serbian operations and Kolwezi (DRC). Zijin's ~40% interest in Kamoa-Kakula is equity-accounted, so its profits appear as investment income rather than revenue. Gold output (~90t in 2025) is growing toward 105t in 2026 and 130–140t by 2028. Lithium (3Q in Argentina, Lakkor Tso in Tibet, Manono North-East in the DRC and hard rock in Hunan) is the third leg, with output up ~5x in H1'26 but exposed to the 2026 lithium-price slide.

Capital allocation balances three uses: RMB25–35bn a year of capex on organic growth, opportunistic M&A (RMB15bn of acquisitions in FY25), and a payout target of ~35%. Large minority interests (RMB62bn at FY25) reflect partly owned mines and Zijin Gold International's outside shareholders.

Competitive landscape

Mining is a price-taking business, so competition is about cost position, reserve life and the ability to grow volumes. In copper, Zijin's 1.09Mt places it among the top five global producers alongside Freeport-McMoRan, BHP and Codelco; in gold its ~90t (~2.9Moz) ranks it in the global top ten behind Newmont, Barrick and Agnico Eagle. Few of those peers are growing. Western majors face declining grades, permitting delays and shareholder pressure to return cash rather than build. Zijin's copper output is planned to rise by ~40–50% by 2028.

Its main competitive disadvantages are jurisdictional and reputational. Several key assets sit in countries with resource nationalism (DRC export rules, Serbian environmental scrutiny, Colombian security), and Chinese ownership can complicate approvals in OECD markets. Domestically, CMOC (copper/cobalt in the DRC) is the closest comparable and a rival for African assets; Shandong Gold and Zhaojin are larger domestic gold peers by reserves in China but have little overseas growth.

Addressable market (TAM)

Zijin's addressable market is the global supply of mined copper, gold and lithium. Refined copper consumption runs at about 27Mt a year, worth ~US$400bn at the current COMEX price of ~US$6.77/lb (~US$14,900/t). Demand is growing at 2–3% a year from electrification, grids, renewables and AI data centres, while mine supply is constrained by falling grades and a decade of under-investment. That supports a structural deficit narrative and high prices.

Annual gold mine supply of ~3,600t is worth ~US$485bn at US$4,195/oz. Supply is broadly flat, and central-bank buying and ETF inflows have driven prices. Lithium is a smaller (~1.5Mt LCE, ~US$27bn) but faster-growing market that is currently oversupplied. Zijin's 2026 targets equal roughly 4–5% of world copper and ~3% of world gold mine supply, so growth comes from volume, not share of a fixed pie.

A global share-taker at a trough multiple; overseas is now the earnings engine

Sany is China's largest construction-machinery maker and one of the world's top five or six OEMs, with 61% of H1'26 revenue earned outside China. Profit quality is improving (H1'26 EBIT +56%), but FX finance costs masked it at the net line. The business

Sany designs, manufactures and sells construction machinery. Excavators are the largest line (~40% of H1'26 revenue), followed by concrete machinery (pumps, mixer trucks), cranes, piling rigs and road machinery. Machines are sold through a network of independent dealers in China and abroad, supplemented by direct sales to large contractors and miners. To support demand, Sany offers customer financing through instalment sales and finance leases. This lifts volumes but leaves sizeable receivables and credit risk on the balance sheet (receivables of RMB40bn, ~164 days of sales, at FY25).

Profitability depends on three levers. Volume drives fixed-cost absorption in large plants. Mix matters because large excavators, mining equipment and overseas units carry higher margins (H1'26 overseas gross margin 32.5%). Pricing discipline is the third lever, which in China depends on how aggressively XCMG, Zoomlion and others chase share. After-market parts and services are a growing, higher-margin, less cyclical revenue stream as the installed base ages.

Globalisation is the strategic core. Overseas revenue was ~US$7.8bn in FY25 (+15%), about 64% of sales, led by Asia-Pacific and fast-growing Africa (+55%). Sany localises production in India, Indonesia, the US, Germany (Putzmeister) and Brazil to shorten delivery times and reduce tariff exposure. Electrification (electric excavators, mixers and trucks) and digitalised 'lighthouse' factories are differentiators that improve cost and position Sany for emission rules in developed markets.

The domestic business is a replacement and infrastructure story rather than a property story. China's 2016–21 boom left a large fleet now reaching replacement age, and water-conservancy, urban-renewal and mining spending support demand. That is why China excavator sales grew 17% in 2025 despite a weak property market.

The business

The business

JL Mag makes sintered NdFeB permanent magnets, the strongest commercially available magnets, which allow smaller, lighter and more efficient electric motors and generators. The process runs from alloy melting and strip casting through hydrogen decrepitation, jet milling, pressing in a magnetic field, sintering, machining and coating, with grain-boundary diffusion for high-coercivity grades. Each customer program needs custom specifications and lengthy qualification, especially in automotive.

End-markets in H1'26 were NEVs and auto parts (56% of revenue: traction motors, EPS, other motors), energy-efficient air-conditioners (24%: inverter compressors), robots and industrial servo motors (6.6%), wind power (6.3%), and consumer electronics and others. Robot-related sales nearly doubled (+96%) and are the fastest-growing line.

Pricing is broadly raw-material cost (PrNd, Dy, Tb oxides and metals) plus a processing fee, adjusted periodically. JL Mag's profit therefore comes from volume and processing margin, plus inventory gains or losses when rare-earth prices move. That explains the swing from a 10.4% gross margin in FY24, when prices fell, to 21.7% in H1'26. Raw materials come from China's state-controlled rare-earth groups. Export licensing since 2025 adds administrative friction for overseas customers but reinforces China's dominance. Working capital is substantial: receivables and inventory each run at ~160 days.

Strategy has three strands. Capacity: from ~40kt in 2025 to a targeted 60kt by end-2027, including the Baotou base next to light rare-earth supply and overseas capacity for customers that want supply outside China. Mix: more output for robots, servo motors and humanoid joint motors, where specifications are tighter and processing fees higher. Technology: grain-boundary diffusion and low-heavy-rare-earth grades reduce its need for dysprosium and terbium, the materials most exposed to export controls.

Competitive landscape

China makes roughly 90% of the world's sintered NdFeB magnets. Domestic competitors include Ningbo Yunsheng, Zhong Ke San Huan, Ningbo Jintian, Yantai Zhenghai, Innuovo (Hengdian DMEGC) and Galaxy Magnetic, plus many smaller producers of lower grades. JL Mag is the largest in high-performance grades and has the deepest automotive qualification list.

Outside China, Proterial (formerly Hitachi Metals) and Shin-Etsu in Japan, TDK and VAC (Germany, building in the US) are established; MP Materials (US) and Neo Performance (Estonia) are building Western supply chains with government support. Those efforts are small relative to China and much higher-cost, but over time they will take share of Western automakers' purchases. JL Mag's counter-strategy is overseas capacity and long-term contracts.

Export controls have changed the competitive map. Since April 2025 China has required export licences for seven medium and heavy rare earths and for magnets that contain them. That has pushed Western automakers to qualify non-Chinese suppliers over time, but in the near term it favours established Chinese producers with licences, long customer relationships and overseas plants, such as JL Mag.

The business

The business

Sieyuan sells grid equipment to four customer groups. State Grid and China Southern Grid buy through centralised competitive tenders. Power generators and renewables developers need substations and grid connections. Industrial users and data centres buy in-house substations. Overseas utilities and EPC contractors made up 29.5% of H1'26 revenue. Revenue follows orders with a 6–18-month lag, so management's 2026 targets of RMB37.5bn of new orders (+30%) and RMB27bn of revenue (+25%) give reasonable visibility.

Primary equipment (switchgear and transformers) forms the profit core. Switchgear was 40% of H1'26 revenue and transformers 21%, both with gross margins around or above 30%. Secondary equipment (protection, automation, compensation) adds higher-value technology content. Energy storage (13% of revenue) is a newer integration business with a gross margin of only 10.5%. It grew 231% in H1'26 and is diluting group margins. EPC projects carry lower margins but help win overseas equipment orders.

The business is asset-light relative to its revenue (PP&E of RMB3.4bn against RMB21.5bn of sales) but working-capital intensive. Grid customers pay in stages, with retention money held until warranty expiry, so receivables run at ~190 days of sales. Sieyuan offsets this with supplier credit (payables ~200 days of cost of sales), and it has remained net cash throughout its growth phase.

Management's strategy has three strands. First, push further into higher-voltage products in China, where qualification is hardest and margins are best. Second, globalise: the planned H-share listing is meant to fund overseas plants and service networks, turning project exports into local market positions in the Middle East, Latin America and Europe. Third, add adjacent products (storage, reactive-power compensation for renewables, data-centre power distribution) that use the same customer relationships. Storage's 10.5% gross margin shows the risk in that third strand: growth there dilutes returns unless it scales.

The business

GigaDevice is a fabless chip designer. It designs memory, microcontroller, sensor and analog chips, outsources wafer fabrication (NOR and MCU wafers to foundries such as SMIC and Hua Hong, DRAM wafers to CXMT) and outsources packaging and testing. It sells through distributors and directly to consumer-electronics, industrial, automotive, PC/server and communications customers, with a large and loyal customer base in China.

SLC NAND and niche DRAM serve set-top boxes, networking, industrial and automotive systems that cannot use leading-edge memory. When prices rise, as they did in 2026 when Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron exited legacy products, revenue and margins explode. Memory gross margin jumped to 67.6% in H1'26 from 42.8% in FY25. When prices fall, the reverse happens, as in FY23, when net profit fell to RMB0.16bn.

Microcontrollers (~12% of revenue) are a steadier, design-win business. GD32 offers hundreds of Arm and RISC-V parts for industrial control, motor drives, consumer devices and increasingly automotive, where qualification cycles are long but customers are sticky. Domestic substitution of STMicro, NXP and Renesas parts is the main growth driver. Sensors and analog chips (~3%) are small.

GigaDevice carries large inventories (~220 days of cost of sales) as a strategic buffer and holds equity stakes in other chip companies. Those stakes generate volatile fair-value gains: more than RMB2bn of H1'26 profit came from this source.

Strategy has three priorities. First, use the 2026 memory upcycle to fund higher-value products: higher-density NOR for AI servers, automotive-grade memory, and DDR4/LPDDR4 parts as the majors exit. Second, push MCUs into automotive and high-performance industrial uses, including RISC-V cores, where design wins last a decade. Third, invest the H-share proceeds in R&D and strategic stakes. Together with record cash flow, the listing lifts net cash to ~RMB22.8bn by FY26E on our estimates.

Competitive landscape

Specialty memory is an oligopoly of mid-sized Asian suppliers. In NOR flash, Winbond (No.1), GigaDevice (No.2, ~18%) and Macronix lead, with Puya, XMC and ISSI behind. In SLC NAND, Kioxia, Winbond and Macronix are the main rivals. In niche DRAM, Nanya, Winbond and ESMT compete as the big three DRAM makers exit DDR4. Product differentiation is limited, so share depends on cost, capacity access and customer relationships. Taiwanese peers trade at ~4x forward earnings, a reminder that the market capitalises peak memory profits cautiously.

In MCUs, GigaDevice competes with STMicroelectronics, NXP, Infineon, Renesas, Microchip and TI globally, and with Chinese rivals such as Nations Technologies, Chipsea and Artery at home. Its advantages are breadth (one of the largest Arm MCU catalogues in China), pin-compatibility with Western parts, and price. Its gaps are automotive qualification depth and global sales coverage. The CXMT link is both a strength (secure DRAM supply) and a risk (related-party dependence, US export-control exposure).

Shortages also reshape customer behaviour. When legacy memory is scarce, buyers put supply security first and qualify second sources. GigaDevice's domestic wafer partners give Chinese customers a local supply chain that is insulated from export controls. If the share it gains in the shortage proves sticky after prices normalise, the memory franchise will be worth more than a cyclical peak; that is the bull case.

Company background

Shenzhen Edge Medical was founded in May 2017 in Longgang, Shenzhen, by Dr Wang Jianchen, who remains chairman and CEO. The company set out to build a full family of endoscopic surgical robots for laparoscopic surgery, rather than a single me-too system. In China and globally that market has been dominated by Intuitive Surgical's da Vinci. Edge developed a multi-port robot (MP1000), a single-port robot (SP1000) and a natural-orifice (bronchoscopy) robot. It became the first Chinese company, and only the second globally, to have all three types approved.

Rather than wait for China's hospital purchase quotas, Edge pursued overseas registrations aggressively. By mid-2026 MP1000 was approved in 37 overseas jurisdictions, and most placements were going abroad. Edge also built a telesurgery capability, with more than 500 remote procedures by end-2025, a selling point in geographically dispersed markets. After two earlier Hong Kong IPO filings (2022, 2023), Edge listed on 8 January 2026 at HK$43.24, raising ~HK$1.1bn with cornerstones including ADIA, UBS Asset Management, OrbiMed and Tencent. It is preparing an A-share listing. By June 2026 it had installed or delivered 158 robots (55 in China, 103 overseas).

The business

Edge designs, manufactures and sells endoscopic surgical robots, used mainly in urology, gynaecology, general and thoracic surgery. Systems made up 99.6% of H1'26 revenue, sold directly to hospitals in China and through distributors abroad (Italy, Spain and other European markets, Asia, the Middle East, Africa and Latin America). Overseas sales were 69.8% of H1'26 revenue (RMB223m), unusual for a Chinese medtech company.

The long-term model is razor and blade. Each robot placed generates recurring revenue from instruments (limited-use, replaced every few procedures), consumables and service contracts, as at Intuitive, where most revenue is recurring. Edge is still early: recurring revenue is below 1% of sales, so revenue today is lumpy and driven by system placements. On our calculation, H1'26 revenue averaged ~RMB5.5m per robot placed, well below typical da Vinci list prices, which is central to Edge's pitch to price-sensitive hospitals.

Gross margin is already high (~64–66%) for a company of its size, and operating costs have fallen as development programmes matured (H1'26 R&D −44%), bringing the company close to breakeven. Production capacity is more than 100 units a year, and a ~350-unit plant is under construction. Working capital is meaningful because overseas distributors receive credit terms: receivables were ~196 days of sales at FY25.

Strategy for the next three years is to turn placements into recurring revenue. That means raising procedure volumes per robot through surgeon training and clinical programmes, growing instrument and service sales, and adding capacity (the ~350-unit plant) so that system prices can fall further without hurting margins. The planned A-share listing would add domestic capital and raise Edge's profile with Chinese hospitals.

Company background

Leader Harmonious Drive Systems (Leaderdrive) was founded in Suzhou in 2011 by Zuo Yuyu, who had spent years developing strain-wave (harmonic) gearing at a time when Chinese robot makers relied almost entirely on imports from Japan's Harmonic Drive Systems. Harmonic reducers are compact, zero-backlash gears that sit in the smaller joints of industrial robots, collaborative robots and, increasingly, humanoid robots. They require extremely precise machining of thin-walled flexible components. Leaderdrive mastered the tooth profile, materials and heat treatment, and built many of its own machine tools to control quality and cost.

As China became the world's largest industrial-robot market, Leaderdrive displaced imports with products priced materially below Japanese equivalents. It listed on Shanghai's STAR Market in August 2020. Profits peaked at RMB189m in FY21, fell to RMB56m in FY24 as robot demand slowed and prices were cut, and recovered in FY25 on humanoid-robot development and industrial share gains. A ~RMB1.4bn placement in 2024 funded new capacity. In 2026 the company announced plans for a Hong Kong H-share listing and a robotics joint venture with SKF. With humanoid makers such as Agibot and UBTech among its customers, the stock has become one of China's purest listed plays on humanoid robots.

The business

Leaderdrive manufactures harmonic reducers (flexspline, circular spline and wave generator) in standard and custom sizes, plus precision metal parts. Together these were 82.7% of H1'26 revenue. Electromechanical actuators (15.7%) integrate reducer, frameless motor, encoder, brake and drive into a complete joint module, which raises content per joint and suits humanoid and collaborative-robot designers who want to buy modules rather than components.

Its edge is manufacturing. The company designs tooth profiles and processes in-house, builds much of its own precision grinding and machining equipment, and controls heat treatment. That lets it sell at materially lower prices than Harmonic Drive Systems while keeping gross margins around 30%. Margins have fallen from ~50% in FY21 as it cut prices to win share and absorbed new capacity.

Customers include Chinese industrial and collaborative robot makers, CNC machine-tool builders, semiconductor and medical equipment makers, and humanoid-robot developers. Humanoids are still a small share of revenue but dominate the investment case: a humanoid can use ~14–20 harmonic reducers. Revenue is small (RMB571m in FY25), so profit is sensitive to utilisation of new capacity and to interest income on its ~RMB2bn of cash, which makes up a meaningful share of pre-tax profit.

Strategy has shifted from components to modules. Electromechanical actuators already contribute ~16% of revenue. The SKF joint venture and the planned H-share listing are meant to fund capacity for humanoid and collaborative-robot joint modules, where content per robot is far higher than for a reducer alone. The trade-off is capital intensity: capex of ~RMB250m a year in our forecasts is large against FY26E revenue of RMB780m, so free cash flow stays negative until volumes arrive.

Competitive landscape

The global benchmark is Harmonic Drive Systems (Japan), which invented commercial strain-wave gearing and still leads in premium industrial and aerospace applications. Nidec-Shimpo is another Japanese supplier. In China, Leaderdrive is the largest of a growing field that includes Laifual, Zhongda Leader, KOFON, Beijing CTKM and Han's Motion (part of Han's Laser). JPMorgan estimates Leaderdrive's share of China's harmonic-reducer market at 30–40%.

Two competitive risks loom over the humanoid opportunity. Designers may favour other transmissions for some joints, such as planetary gears, RV reducers (Nabtesco, Shuanghuan) or planetary roller screws. And large robot makers, including Tesla, may produce reducers in-house or squeeze suppliers on price as volumes grow. Leaderdrive's lead rests on precision, yield and scale, but harmonic reducers are increasingly a contested component.

Customer qualification is the main barrier to entry. Robot makers test reducers for accuracy, stiffness and life over thousands of hours, and humanoid developers now run similar programmes. Leaderdrive's long record with Chinese industrial-robot makers gives it a head start, but humanoid designers are qualifying several suppliers at once.

Addressable market (TAM)

China installs about 300k industrial robots a year (IFR), the world's largest market. Each uses several harmonic reducers in its wrist and smaller joints, alongside RV reducers in larger joints. Collaborative robots, semiconductor equipment and medical devices add further demand. This core market is growing at mid-single to low-double digits.

Humanoids could be transformational. At ~14–20 harmonic reducers per robot, annual production of one million humanoids would require 14–20 million reducers, many times today's market. Humanoid output is currently only in the tens of thousands, however, and timing, design choices and price points are uncertain. The valuation depends on that future market, not on today's addressable market.

We think about humanoids in stages. Today's pilot volumes are worth little: at an illustrative RMB1,000–2,000 per reducer, 50,000 humanoids a year would need RMB0.7–2.0bn of harmonic reducers across the whole industry. A million-unit market would be worth RMB14–40bn a year, but prices per reducer would probably fall as volumes rise. At ~205x FY27E P/E, Leaderdrive's share price already discounts something close to the later stages.

Company background

Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt was founded in 2002 in Tongxiang, Zhejiang, by Chen Xuehua, who still chairs the company. It began as a cobalt refiner. Huayou bought the Arcadia lithium mine in Zimbabwe and control of cathode maker Bamo in 2021. It runs precursor and cathode joint ventures with Korean partners, a recycling arm, and has agreed to acquire Atlantic Lithium (Ghana). The result is one of the most vertically integrated battery-materials groups in the world, and one of the most capital-intensive.

The business

Huayou earns money at each step from mine to cathode. Upstream, it mines cobalt and copper in the DRC and lithium in Zimbabwe, and processes Indonesian laterite nickel into MHP. Midstream, it refines nickel, cobalt and lithium into battery-grade chemicals. Downstream, it makes ternary (NCM) precursors and cathodes, and some LFP, for cell makers. Recycling closes the loop. In FY25, nickel products were RMB25.9bn of revenue, cathode materials RMB15.0bn, and nickel intermediates RMB11.8bn, with cobalt, copper, lithium, precursors and trading making up the rest.

The profit pool sits upstream. Indonesian HPAL produces nickel at among the lowest cash costs globally, and when nickel or cobalt prices rise, Huayou's mining and refining margins expand quickly. Processing earns steadier fees but thinner margins. Integration lowers cost and secures supply, but it also concentrates risk. Results depend on four commodity prices (nickel, cobalt, lithium, copper) and on input costs, notably sulfur and sulfuric acid for HPAL. Sulfur spikes forced maintenance at Huafei in 2026. Cobalt volumes are subject to DRC export quotas, and shipments fell 24% in H1'26.

The model is capital-hungry. PP&E has grown to RMB74bn, capex reached RMB16bn TTM for Pomalaa, and net debt is ~RMB54bn, about 3x EBITDA. Minority interests are sizeable because Indonesian projects are jointly owned. Customers are major Korean and Chinese cell makers. Many of the relationships are formalised in joint ventures, which give Huayou volume visibility but limit pricing power.

The plan for the next three years is to finish the Indonesian build-out and then harvest it. Pomalaa adds 120ktpa of MHP capacity as it ramps through 2026–27. We assume capex falls from ~RMB15bn in FY26E to ~RMB8bn by FY28E once it is complete, which is what turns free cash flow positive in our model. The second priority is selling more nickel outside China's LFP-dominated market, mainly to Korean and European cell makers. US foreign-entity sourcing rules limit how much Huayou can sell into US supply chains.

Downstream, cathode and precursor demand grows with EV and storage batteries, but nickel-based chemistries are losing share to LFP in China. Huayou's addressable market is therefore growing more slowly than the overall battery market. Its growth comes from Indonesian volume and share gains with overseas cell makers, rather than from rising nickel intensity.

A rough sizing shows Huayou's weight in nickel. Its three HPAL projects total ~300ktpa of nickel-in-MHP capacity at 100% ownership (less on an attributable basis), equal to roughly 8% of global nickel supply. That is large enough to matter to the market but not to set prices. Indonesian supply growth has pushed nickel prices down toward the costs of higher-cost producers elsewhere, so Huayou's returns depend on staying at the bottom of the cost curve rather than on a price recovery.

The business

Tianqi has three earnings streams. First, its share of Greenbushes mining profits. The mine sells spodumene concentrate to its two owners at market-linked prices, and Tianqi resells part of its share as concentrate (42% of H1'26 revenue). Second, conversion. Tianqi turns the rest into lithium carbonate and hydroxide at plants in Shehong (Sichuan), Zhangjiagang (Jiangsu) and Kwinana (Australia), and sells them to cathode and battery makers (58% of revenue). Third, equity income from its SQM stake, which taps Chile's low-cost Atacama brine.

Mining is where the money is. Greenbushes delivered a 74% mining gross margin in H1'26, and its cash cost sits in the bottom quartile of the industry. Conversion margins are thinner and volatile. Because Tianqi buys concentrate at lagged market prices and sells chemicals at spot, falling prices can generate inventory losses, as in FY24. Kwinana, Australia's first lithium hydroxide plant, has struggled with commissioning and ran at ~15% of nameplate in the June 2026 quarter.

Earnings are almost entirely a function of the lithium price, volumes at Greenbushes (where the CGP3 expansion was interrupted by a fire in June 2026), and SQM's results. Development projects include the Cuola spodumene mine in Sichuan. The balance sheet has been repaired since 2021, but the A-share and H-share valuations differ widely (H at a ~44% discount).

Competitive landscape

Lithium supply is split between hard-rock spodumene (Australia, Africa, China) and brine (Chile, Argentina, China's Qinghai and Tibet), with lepidolite in Jiangxi as a high-cost swing source. Tianqi's peers include Albemarle (its Greenbushes partner), SQM, Pilbara Minerals, Mineral Resources and Rio Tinto (which bought Arcadium). In China its rivals are Ganfeng, Yahua, Zangge, Sinomine and battery makers that have integrated upstream, such as CATL in Yichun.

The industry is a price-taker, so cost position is everything. Greenbushes is among the lowest-cost sources worldwide, which protects Tianqi in downturns and gives it high leverage in upturns. Its relative weaknesses are a minority position in its key asset (Albemarle and IGO are co-owners), under-performing downstream conversion, and limited growth projects of its own compared with Ganfeng or Pilbara. African supply (Zimbabwe, Mali, Ghana) and Chinese lepidolite restarts are the marginal tonnes that set prices.

The cost curve is the right lens. Greenbushes concentrate is among the cheapest spodumene in the world to produce, while Jiangxi lepidolite and many African and Australian hard-rock mines sit at the top of the curve. When prices fall, those high-cost tonnes are the first to close, as they did in 2024. Tianqi's conversion plants are less advantaged: Chinese converters run on thin processing margins, and Kwinana's problems have made Western conversion look expensive.

Company background

Ganfeng Lithium was founded in 2000 in Xinyu, Jiangxi, by Li Liangbin (chairman) and Wang Xiaoshen. It started as a maker of lithium metal and specialty lithium compounds, and it remains the world's largest lithium-metal producer, a material used in pharmaceuticals, alloys and next-generation batteries. It listed in Shenzhen in 2010 and in Hong Kong in 2018, and used both markets to fund a decade of resource acquisitions.

Ganfeng built one of the most diversified lithium resource portfolios worldwide. It holds Mt Marion spodumene in Australia (with Mineral Resources), the Cauchari-Olaroz brine in Argentina (with Lithium Argentina; first production in 2023), the Mariana brine (Argentina), the Goulamina spodumene mine in Mali (fully owned after buying out Leo Lithium in 2024; production from late 2024), and lepidolite and brine assets in China. Not every bet has worked: Mexico revoked its Sonora lithium-clay concessions in 2023, and Mali's military government has tightened mining rules. In parallel, Ganfeng LiEnergy has grown into a sizeable battery business making consumer cells, storage systems and power batteries. It is one of China's leading developers of solid-state batteries, with 400Wh/kg cells validated and 500Wh/kg cells in small-batch production in 2026.

The business

Ganfeng has two businesses. The lithium business (RMB14.2bn, 62% of H1'26 revenue) mines and buys spodumene, brine and lepidolite and converts it into lithium carbonate, hydroxide, chloride, metal and organolithium compounds for cathode, battery, pharmaceutical and industrial customers. It earns a conversion margin on everything it processes and a mining margin on the share it mines itself. Self-sufficiency is rising as Goulamina, Mariana and Cauchari-Olaroz ramp.

The battery business (RMB7.8bn, 34% of revenue; about 20GWh in H1'26) makes small-format consumer cells (1.5m cells a day across two bases), storage cells and 5MWh containerised systems, and power batteries for commercial vehicles and two-wheelers. Margins are lower than in lithium at the top of the cycle but more stable, and the business gives Ganfeng a captive outlet for its lithium. The solid-state programme (lithium-metal anodes, sulfide electrolytes) targets premium EVs, eVTOLs and robots. It is pre-revenue in scale but strategically important.

Economically, Ganfeng is more diversified than Tianqi but has a higher-cost, more geopolitically exposed resource base and more debt. FY25 gross margin was only 14.6%, recovering to 31.2% in H1'26. Heavy development capex in Mali and Argentina (RMB7–9bn a year over FY23–25) pushed net debt to ~RMB29bn. Recycling and lithium-sulfide supply for solid-state electrolytes add smaller revenue streams.

Ganfeng's strategy is to integrate both upstream and downstream. Upstream, it is lifting self-sufficiency in lithium resources as Goulamina, Mariana and Cauchari-Olaroz ramp, which should lower its cost position through the cycle. Downstream, it uses the battery business and solid-state technology to capture more of the value chain, accepting lower margins today for a position in next-generation cells. We assume capex slows to RMB5–6bn a year in FY26–28E, from RMB7–9bn in FY23–25, which is what allows free cash flow to turn positive.

The business

Glass fibre is made by melting silica-rich minerals in continuous tank furnaces and drawing the melt through platinum-rhodium bushings into fine filaments. Furnaces run non-stop for 8–10 years before a cold repair, so the business is capital- and energy-intensive with high fixed costs. Scale, furnace efficiency, energy prices and utilisation determine cost position, and Jushi is the industry cost leader with its own mineral mines, large furnaces and automated lines.

Products fall into two families. Reinforcement fibre (direct and assembled roving, chopped strands, mats and fabrics), the bulk of volume, goes into wind-turbine blades, construction materials, pipes and tanks, automotive thermoplastics and consumer goods. Pricing follows the capacity cycle and was flat in H1'26. Electronic yarn and woven electronic cloth go to copper-clad-laminate makers (Kingboard, Shengyi, Nan Ya and others), who make the base material for printed circuit boards. This is the profit engine today: AI servers and high-speed networking boards use more layers and more cloth, standard 7628 cloth averaged RMB6.02/m in H1'26 (+49%), and further 15–20% increases followed in September.

Jushi sells mostly direct to industrial customers in China and abroad (Egypt and US plants serve Europe and North America). Working capital is moderate (receivables ~116 days, inventory ~100 days at FY25), and the balance sheet carries ~RMB11bn of net debt from successive capacity builds. The strategic task now is to move up the e-cloth value chain into thin and low-dielectric grades, where Japan's Nittobo leads. Jushi's low-dielectric products are in customer certification.

Competitive landscape

The business

Horizon calls its model 'ARM + Android' for intelligent driving. It sells three layers that customers can combine as they wish. Journey system-on-chips are designed in-house and manufactured by foundries; they are sold with reference solutions for ADAS and highway/urban NOA. HSD (Horizon SuperDrive) is a complete urban driving system, software included, for automakers without large in-house teams. Licensing covers processor IP, algorithms, the toolchain and foundation models, for OEMs and Tier-1s that build their own stacks.

The economics differ sharply by layer. Products and solutions (45% of H1'26 revenue, RMB926m, +15%) carry hardware-like gross margins of roughly 35–40%. Licensing and services (55%, RMB1.13bn, +53%) earned a 90% gross margin in H1'26. Licence income scales with partners' vehicle volumes and new programmes, while unit shipments (2.22m Journey SoCs in H1'26, +12%) drive product revenue. Group gross margin was 66% in H1'26.

Customers are almost every major Chinese automaker plus a growing list of international brands and Tier-1s. Toyota programmes, Denso and AUMOVIO are developing on Journey 6B. Design wins for nearly 500 models (about 130 with advanced NOA) provide a multi-year revenue pipeline. The cost base is dominated by R&D (RMB5.2bn in FY25, more than 1.3x revenue), which funds new chip generations, HSD and foundation models. Management targets breakeven in 2028. Working capital is significant (receivables ~180 days and inventory ~290 days at FY25) because chips are built ahead of OEM ramps.

Management's priorities for 2026–28 are to scale HSD on mid-priced cars, keep licensing revenue growing faster than chip shipments, and widen the customer base outside China through Toyota programmes, Denso and AUMOVIO. Each raises content per vehicle and the share of high-margin software and licence revenue. R&D is the swing factor. Competitive landscape

Company background

On 8 January 2026 it became Hong Kong's first listed LLM developer (IPO at HK$116.20). A Shanghai A-share listing is planned.

The business

Z.AI develops and sells the GLM family of foundation models (language, coding, vision, agents). It monetises them in two main ways. The cloud/API business (86.5% of H1'26 revenue, up from 26% in FY25) charges developers and enterprises per token through its own platform, sells fixed-price coding subscription plans (popular with developers globally), and earns revenue shares when GLM is offered on hyperscaler clouds (AWS, Alibaba Cloud, Huawei Cloud). The on-premise business (13.5% of H1'26 revenue but 74% in FY25) installs models on customers' own servers for state-owned enterprises, government and regulated industries. These deals are larger but lumpier, and often run on domestic hardware.

The economics are those of a compute-intensive software business at an early stage. Gross margin fell from ~50% to 26.4% in H1'26 as low-margin cloud traffic grew. Every token served consumes GPU or NPU time, and price competition in China has been fierce. Moving inference to domestic chips cut unit cost by 80%, which is key to margin recovery. R&D, mostly training compute and researcher pay, exceeded RMB3bn in FY25, about four times revenue.

Management targets annual recurring revenue (ARR) of US$3.0bn by end-2026, roughly ten times the H1'26 revenue run-rate. Whether that is reached depends on coding-plan subscriptions and international API demand. The ~US$9.5bn raised since the IPO gives Z.AI one of the largest war chests among Chinese AI labs, to fund GLM-6.0 and compute.

The strategic question is how Z.AI spends its war chest. Management's priorities are next-generation models (GLM-6.0), domestic compute and international growth through coding plans and hyperscaler marketplaces. We model capex of RMB1.5–3.0bn a year and operating cash outflows of RMB4–6bn a year. On that basis the ~RMB38,314m of net cash we expect at FY26E covers several years of investment without further raises.

Competitive landscape

China's foundation-model market is crowded. DeepSeek's open-weight models set a low price benchmark in 2025. Alibaba (Qwen), ByteDance (Doubao/Seed), Tencent (Hunyuan) and Baidu (ERNIE) bundle models with cloud services and can subsidise them. Independent labs such as Moonshot (Kimi), MiniMax and StepFun compete for developers and enterprises. Globally, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google lead on frontier capability. The domestic API market has been in a price war, though DeepSeek's August 2026 price increase hints at rationalisation.

Z.AI's differentiation is coding and agentic capability, the trust of enterprise and government buyers (important for on-premise deals), its distribution through international hyperscalers, and now its balance sheet. Its disadvantages are the Entity-List constraint on hardware, dependence on expensive compute, and the risk that hyperscalers' own models commoditise third-party APIs.

Benchmarks change monthly, so the lasting competitive assets are distribution, developer loyalty and cost per token. Z.AI's coding subscriptions and integrations with developer tools give it a direct relationship with users, and its move to domestic inference chips cuts unit cost. Both matter more than a temporary lead on a leaderboard.

The business

MiniMax runs two businesses on top of its own foundation models. The Open Platform and enterprise services (63.4% of H1'26 revenue, US$74m, +703%) sell API access to text, speech and video models, priced per token, per character of speech or per video, plus subscriptions and licences for developers and enterprises. AI-native consumer apps (36.6%, US$43m) monetise through subscriptions and in-app credits. These are Talkie and Xingye (AI companions), Hailuo AI (video generation) and MiniMax Voice/Audio.

The consumer apps gave MiniMax scale (236m users across 200+ countries by FY25) and data, but they are expensive: user acquisition and inference costs are high, and companion apps face content-regulation risk. The 2026 shift toward enterprise and API revenue is strategically sensible, because marginal costs are lower and customers stickier. Gross margin was 25.4% in FY25 and 17.9% in H1'26, still low because of heavy inference costs and price competition.

R&D (US$297m in H1'26) is about 2.5x revenue and the dominant cost, mostly training compute. MiniMax is known for cost-efficient architectures, but it competes against far larger budgets. Capex jumped in 2026 (US$188m TTM) as it built its own compute. Cash of ~US$1.2bn at June 2026 is modest against an annual burn of ~US$0.6bn.

Management's strategy has three parts: keep its models near the frontier at a fraction of rivals' budgets through efficient architectures; grow the higher-margin Open Platform faster than consumer apps; and build some of its own compute to cut inference costs (capex of US$250–300m a year in our forecasts). The balance sheet is the constraint. With ~US$1,086m of net cash at June 2026 and annual burn of ~US$0.6bn, MiniMax will probably need to raise capital again by 2028 unless losses narrow faster than we expect.

Methodology

Scope. Each of the 17 companies in the source pack gets an initiation of up to five pages: a summary page with rating, thesis, catalysts, risks and key financials; at least two pages on company background, the business, the competitive landscape and the addressable market (TAM); a financial analysis covering the income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement and key ratios; and a valuation. They are concise initiations, not full models.

Data. Historical financials (FY23–25 and TTM) come from company filings as compiled by StockAnalysis.com (S&P Global Market Intelligence data) and from H1'26 interim reports. Consensus estimates, consensus targets and peer multiples are from StockAnalysis/S&P Global as of 12 October 2026 unless a broker is named. Share prices, 52-week ranges, FX and gold/copper futures are from Interactive Brokers (12-Oct-2026). Industry figures (CCMA, SNE Research, Wood Mackenzie, Argus, TrendForce, Frost & Sullivan, IFR, Prismark) are cited as reported by the sources named in each note.

Estimates. FY26–28E figures are our own simplified projections of revenue, gross margin and attributable net profit (adjusted net profit for loss-makers). EPS uses current shares outstanding. Dividends assume the payout ratios stated in each report. Cash flow and balance sheet are projected with a simple roll-forward: operating cash flow = net profit + D&A (grown at 10–12% a year, faster for capacity builders) − the increase in net working capital (receivables + inventory − payables, at the FY25 ratio to revenue or a stated incremental intensity); free cash flow = OCF − our capex assumption; year-end net cash = prior net cash + FCF − dividends paid (prior-year DPS) + equity raised. For companies that listed or recapitalised in 2026 (Horizon, Edge Medical, Z.AI, MiniMax) the FY26E roll-forward starts from the June 2026 balance sheet. Historical ratios are computed from reported statements: ROE and ROA on average balances; working-capital days on year-end balances (receivables on revenue, inventory and payables on cost of sales); net debt = total debt (including leases) − cash and short-term investments.

Valuation. Profitable companies use target P/E multiples on FY27E EPS, cross-checked against listed peers and the bear/bull scenarios shown. Loss-making growth companies use EV/sales on FY27E (Horizon, Edge Medical) or FY28E revenue discounted two years at 15% (Z.AI, MiniMax), plus net cash. Leaderdrive uses capitalised FY30E earnings discounted three years at 10%. H-share targets convert RMB values at HK$1 = RMB0.8531; MiniMax at US$1 = HK$7.8475.

Ratings. Buy: implied 12-month return ≥ +20%. Hold: between −10% and +20%. Sell: ≤ −10%. Ratings are derived mechanically from the target price.
